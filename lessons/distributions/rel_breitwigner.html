Relativistic Breit-Wigner

scipy.stats.rel_breitwigner

Intuition

Physicists use this to model the measured mass of a short-lived particle resonance: quantum uncertainty smears the particle's 'true' mass into this peaked curve, whose width relates directly to how quickly the particle decays -- the default rho=36 mirrors the real Z boson's mass-to-width ratio.

Formula

\[ f(x;\rho) = \frac{k}{(x^2-\rho^2)^2+\rho^2}, \quad k = \frac{2\sqrt2\,\rho^2\sqrt{\rho^2+1}}{\pi\sqrt{\rho^2+\rho\sqrt{\rho^2+1}}} \]
parameterdefault used heremeaning
rho36ratio of the resonance mass to its decay width, M0/Gamma

Use cases

  • Modeling particle resonance mass measurements in high-energy physics (e.g. the Z boson)
  • Fitting spectral line shapes in nuclear and particle physics

A funny example

A science-fair project 'weighs' a wobbly, fast-spinning top by measuring it many times. Because it's spinning and unstable, each measurement comes out a little different, clustering around its true mass in this specific peaked shape.

What's the probability the measured mass falls within 5 units of the resonance peak (rho)?

Solve it with scipy.stats

import scipy.stats as stats
import numpy as np

dist = stats.rel_breitwigner(rho=36)
print('P(|mass - peak| < 5):', round(dist.cdf(41) - dist.cdf(31), 4))
print('mean mass:', round(dist.mean(), 4))
P(|mass - peak| < 5): 0.9375 mean mass: 35.6921
