Crystal Ball

scipy.stats.crystalball

Intuition

Named after the crystal-ball fortune-telling prop, this distribution is a normal curve that has been given a heavy power-law tail on one side, exactly the shape seen when a detector's Gaussian measurement noise combines with real physical energy loss.

Formula

\[ f(x;\beta,m) = \begin{cases} N\exp(-x^2/2), & x > -\beta \\ N\,A\,(B-x)^{-m}, & x \le -\beta \end{cases} \]
parameterdefault used heremeaning
beta2distance (in std devs) from the mean where the curve switches from Gaussian to power-law tail
m3power-law exponent of the tail, m > 1

Use cases

  • Modeling particle decay mass spectra with radiative energy loss in physics
  • Signal peaks with an asymmetric heavy tail from measurement artifacts

A funny example

A carnival strongman game measures how hard people hit a bell -- most hits scatter symmetrically around the average strength, but every so often someone's mallet skids and the reading drops way lower than a normal swing would ever produce.

What's the probability a reading falls more than 2 units below the peak (in the power-law tail), versus staying in the normal-looking bulk?

Solve it with scipy.stats

import scipy.stats as stats
import numpy as np

dist = stats.crystalball(beta=2, m=3)
print('P(X < -2):', round(dist.cdf(-2), 4))
print('mean:', round(dist.mean(), 4))
P(X < -2): 0.0398 mean: -0.0862
Theoretical shape at the default parameters above (blue = density/PMF, orange = CDF).
